The reward_fn fixture is not the real grader: it is a deterministic SHA-256 hash of the response string mapped into {0.0, 0.1, …, 0.9}, chosen so the group-normalization test has spread in its rewards without needing sympy. And group_size is 4 over 8 rollouts, so the group test really does exercise two groups rather than degenerating to one.

2 · Reasoning with language models

Two things change relative to every prior assignment, and the handout is unusually explicit about both. You stop using your own model, because a 100M-parameter transformer trained on TinyStories has no mathematical reasoning to elicit — you cannot RL your way to competition math from a model that never had it. And you stop using cross-entropy as the yardstick, because the whole premise now is the gap between likelihood and downstream behaviour. Qwen 2.5 Math 1.5B Base (Yang et al. 2024) was continually pretrained on synthetic math, which is exactly why it has latent ability that post-training can surface.

The intellectual arc packed into §2.2 runs: scratchpads (Nye et al. 2021) showed a model can be finetuned to externalize intermediate steps; chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al. 2023) showed you can get the same for free from a big enough model; STaR turned it into a bootstrap loop — sample chains, keep the ones that reach the right answer, finetune on those, repeat — which is expert iteration with a string-match verifier standing in for a search oracle; and then o1, R1 and Kimi k1.5 replaced the filter-and-finetune step with an actual policy gradient. You will implement that whole ladder in order, which is the best argument for doing §5 before §7 even though GRPO is the headline.

The R1-Zero prompt. Everything in the required assignment uses the prompt in cs336_alignment/prompts/r1_zero.prompt — copy it from the file, not from the PDF, which is exactly why the file exists. It instructs the assistant to think inside <think> </think> and answer inside <answer> </answer>, and it pre-fills the opening <think> so the model starts mid-reasoning. Two tags, two jobs: <answer> gives the grader something to parse, and </answer> gives vLLM a stop string, which is what keeps rollouts from running to 1024 tokens every time.

The handout then undercuts its own choice, and this is worth reading carefully because it sets up the last ablation. Liu et al. 2025 (Dr. GRPO) found that prompting Qwen 2.5 Math with nothing but the bare question starts far higher and takes the R1-Zero prompt 100+ RL steps just to catch up — evidence the model was pretrained on question-answer pairs in roughly that shape. The staff pick R1-Zero anyway, on the grounds that it improves visibly fast and therefore makes a correct implementation obvious within a handful of steps. You verify the claim yourself in grpo_prompt_ablation.

3 · Measuring zero-shot MATH performance

3.1 · vLLM for offline inference. RL is generation-bound: every GRPO step samples hundreds of complete solutions before a single gradient flows. Writing your own sampler (as you did in A1) is fine for a demo and hopeless here, so the assignment hands you vLLM — continuous batching, paged KV cache (Kwon et al. 2023), the whole machine L10 takes apart. The starter snippet in the handout is lifted from vLLM's own offline-inference example: build SamplingParams, build LLM(model=...), call llm.generate(prompts, sampling_params), read output.outputs[0].text.

3.2 · The reward function. Math answers cannot be string-compared: the gold label 0.5 and the model's 1/2 are the same answer. The course hands you a parser rather than making you write one — cs336_alignment/drgrpo_grader.py, about a thousand lines lifted from Dr. GRPO, with LaTeX normalization, sympy equivalence and a fast path. The entry point is r1_zero_reward_fn(response, ground_truth), which returns a dict with "format_reward", "answer_reward" and "reward". Read its first line before you run anything: the format gate is the literal substring "</think> <answer>", one space, plus "</answer>" somewhere after. Also note the asymmetry that the write-up asks you to explain — a formatted-but-wrong answer gets format_reward 1.0 and total reward 0.0, deliberately, so a model cannot farm reward by emitting well-formed nonsense.

Problem (math_baseline): 4 points

Deliverable: an evaluation script, plus a three-part write-up on where the base model and the parser each fail.

Part (a) is the script: load validation.jsonl, format each example with the R1-Zero prompt, generate, score, and — the part people skip and regret — serialize examples, generations and per-example scores to disk. Parts (b) and (c) require you to go back and read those generations, and re-running 5,000 rollouts because you only kept the aggregate is a waste of an hour. The handout suggests factoring the work into evaluate_vllm(vllm_model, reward_fn, prompts, eval_sampling_params), and it is worth taking that suggestion literally: you will call this function again in sft_experiment, in the EI loop and inside the GRPO train loop's validation pass.

Part (b) is the real content. Bucket the 5,000 generations three ways — format 1 / answer 1, format 1 / answer 0, format 0 / answer 0 — then read at least ten from each of the failure buckets and argue whether the fault is the model's or the parser's. Both happen. Format-0 is usually the base model genuinely never closing </think>, or drifting into a fresh User: turn, or hitting the 1024-token cap mid-derivation; but it is also sometimes the parser's exact-space requirement rejecting </think><answer> or </think>\n<answer>. Format-1/answer-0 splits between honest arithmetic errors and equivalence the grader misses — an interval written differently, an unsimplified radical, a unit tacked on. The point of the exercise is calibration: your entire RL signal is this function, and you should know its failure modes before you spend 50 H100-hours optimizing against it.

4 · Supervised finetuning for MATH

Algorithm 1 in the handout is four lines and holds no surprises: sample a batch of (question, response) pairs, cross-entropy on the response, step. What makes it interesting is what the responses are. You are not finetuning the model to emit correct answers; you are finetuning it to emit a chain of thought and then an answer, using traces distilled from DeepSeek R1 in MATH/sft.jsonl. Distillation of a reasoning process, not of an answer key.

The handout also explains why SFT and RL are treated as separate phases here rather than composed as they are in practice: at 1.5B parameters the effects do not stack visibly, so chaining them would only add compute and confusion. Two motivations for the ordering survive anyway — SFT needs annotated traces where RL needs only an answer key, and RL can find policies better than anything in the SFT set.

4.1 · HuggingFace mechanics. Load with torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16 and attn_implementation="flash_attention_2", forward with model(input_ids).logits, save with save_pretrained(save_directory=output_dir) under /data/yourusername (checkpoints are large), and save the tokenizer alongside so the directory is self-contained. The section closes with a page on gradient accumulation — divide the loss by gradient_accumulation_steps before backward(), call optimizer.step() and zero_grad() every k microbatches — which matters because both *_microbatch_train_step problems are graded on getting exactly this scaling right.

Problem (tokenize_prompt_and_output): Prompt and output tokenization · 2 points

Deliverable: tokenize prompt and response separately, concatenate, pad, and build a response mask.

This is the boring function that everything else silently depends on, and it is where most of the assignment's off-by-one bugs live. You tokenize prompt and output separately — not the concatenated string — because you need to know where the boundary is; a joint tokenization can merge the last prompt token with the first response token and you lose the split. Then you concatenate, pad to the batch max, and produce three tensors of width max(prompt_and_output_lens) - 1: input_ids is the concatenation with the final token dropped, labels is the same shifted left by one, and response_mask is 1 exactly on the response positions of the labels. That last clause is the trap. The mask indexes labels, not input_ids, so it is shifted too; mask the input positions instead and every loss you compute for the rest of the assignment is off by one token, which is subtle enough to pass a smoke test and wrong enough to ruin a training run. Padding must be masked to 0 as well.

  • tests/test_sft.py — test_tokenize_prompt_and_output snapshots all three tensors against three short prompt/output pairs; needs the Qwen tokenizer from the cluster path

Problem (compute_entropy): Per-token entropy · 1 point

Deliverable: entropy of the next-token distribution at every position, from logits.

One line of math — H(p) = −Σ p(x) log p(x) over the vocabulary — and one instruction that carries the point: do it in a numerically stable way with logsumexp. Naively exponentiating raw logits over a 150K-token vocabulary in bfloat16 overflows. The stable form computes logZ = logsumexp(logits, dim=-1), then H = logZ - sum(softmax(logits) * logits, dim=-1), which never materializes an unnormalized exponential. Input is (batch, seq, vocab), output is (batch, seq) — you reduce the vocabulary axis only. Entropy is not part of any loss; it is the diagnostic you will stare at for the rest of the assignment, because a collapsing entropy curve is how over-confident RL announces itself before the reward curve does.

  • tests/adapters.py · run_compute_entropy — (logits: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor
  • tests/test_sft.py — test_compute_entropy snapshots the output for a seeded (2, 10, 100) random logit tensor; pure CPU, runs anywhere

Problem (get_response_log_probs): Response log-probs (and entropy) · 2 points

Deliverable: per-token conditional log-probabilities from a causal LM, optionally with per-token entropy.

The workhorse. Run the model, take logits, log-softmax over the vocabulary, and gather the entry corresponding to each label — log p(x_t | x_<t) at every position. Return a dict with key "log_probs", plus "token_entropy" when return_token_entropy=True, both shaped (batch, seq). Two notes. The adapter's type annotation says -> torch.Tensor and is simply wrong — the docstring and the test both want a dict; trust the docstring. And read the second half of the docstring carefully: nothing is masked here. Prompt and padding positions come back with real numbers in them, and it is the training loop's job to mask them. People who helpfully zero out the prompt inside this function fail the snapshot and then break the entropy statistics later.

Use torch.gather on the log-softmax rather than indexing with fancy tensors, and be aware that this is the memory peak of a training step: log-softmax over (batch, seq, 150K) in fp32 is larger than the model. When you need old log-probs for off-policy GRPO you call the same function under torch.inference_mode().

  • tests/adapters.py · run_get_response_log_probs — (model, input_ids, labels, return_token_entropy: bool), returns dict[str, torch.Tensor]
  • tests/test_sft.py — test_get_response_log_probs calls it with return_token_entropy=True and snapshots both keys; loads the real Qwen model, so cluster-only

Problem (masked_normalize): Masked normalize · 1 point

Deliverable: sum a tensor over a dimension, counting only masked-in elements, then divide by a constant.

Six lines: (tensor * mask).sum(dim) / normalize_constant, with dim=None meaning sum everything. The reason it is a graded problem at all is that it is one half of the length-normalization debate you will run as an experiment in §8 — this is the "sum and divide by a fixed constant" branch, against masked_mean's "divide by the actual token count" branch. Choosing between them changes which tokens get gradient weight, and think_about_length_normalization asks you to predict the difference before measuring it.

Verify the argument order against the adapter, not the PDF. The handout's recommended signature is (tensor, mask, normalize_constant, dim=None); the actual adapter is (tensor, mask, dim=None, normalize_constant=1.0). The handout's later worked example calls it with a keyword constant_normalizer=, which does not exist under either spelling. Keyword-call it as the adapter declares it and the discrepancy is harmless.

  • tests/adapters.py · run_masked_normalize — (tensor, mask, dim: int | None = None, normalize_constant: float = 1.0) -> torch.Tensor
  • tests/test_sft.py — four snapshots: test_masked_normalize_dim0, _dim1, _dimlast, _dimNone, all with normalize_constant=10.0 and a random boolean mask

Problem (sft_microbatch_train_step): Microbatch train step · 3 points

Deliverable: one forward-and-backward on a microbatch — masked NLL, normalized, scaled for gradient accumulation, backpropagated.

The three points are for getting the scaling right, because everything else is arithmetic. Negate the log-probs, sum them over response tokens only via masked_normalize, divide by gradient_accumulation_steps, call loss.backward() inside the function, and return the scaled scalar plus a metadata dict. Two things people get wrong. They forget the division, which multiplies the effective learning rate by the accumulation count and produces a run that diverges for no visible reason. And they call zero_grad() here — this function must not; accumulation across microbatches is the entire point, and the ten-step test exists precisely to catch it.

That test is worth understanding: test_sft_microbatch_train_step_10_steps calls the step ten times on the same policy_log_probs tensor without ever zeroing, stacks the ten gradients, and snapshots the stack. If your gradients are being cleared, or scaled inconsistently, the stack is wrong even when a single step passes.

  • tests/adapters.py · run_sft_microbatch_train_step — (policy_log_probs, response_mask, gradient_accumulation_steps: int, normalize_constant: int | None = 1.0) -> tuple[torch.Tensor, dict[str, torch.Tensor]]
  • tests/test_sft.py — test_sft_microbatch_train_step snapshots {loss, policy_log_probs_grad}; test_sft_microbatch_train_step_10_steps snapshots the accumulated stack. Fixtures: gradient_accumulation_steps=2, normalize_constant=10.0

Problem (log_generations): Logging generations · 1 point

Deliverable: a function that samples from the current policy on validation prompts and logs what came out.

The cheapest point in the assignment and the one that saves you the most time later. The handout lists six things to log per example: prompt, generated response, ground truth, the reward breakdown (format / answer / total), the average token entropy of the response, and average response length split three ways — overall, correct-only, incorrect-only. That last split is the diagnostic that catches length hacking, the classic RL-on-reasoning failure where the model discovers that rambling correlates with reward and response length climbs while accuracy does not. Build this before sft_experiment, not after, and call it from every loop you write afterwards.

  • Log to wandb (already a dependency); §4.3 gives the define_metric incantation for separate train and eval x-axes.

Problem (sft_experiment): Run SFT on the MATH dataset · 2 points · 2 H100 hrs

Deliverable: validation-accuracy curves across five SFT dataset sizes, plus a second run on a correctness-filtered dataset with its size reported.

Part 1: run SFT on {128, 256, 512, 1024} unique examples and on the full set, tuning learning rate and batch size until the full-dataset run clears 15% validation accuracy. Part 2: filter sft.jsonl down to only those examples whose response actually produces the correct answer under r1_zero_reward_fn, train on that, report both the filtered size and the accuracy, and compare. The expected finding is the one STaR is built on — a smaller, verified dataset beats a larger, noisy one — and it is the direct motivation for §5, where you generate the data instead of being given it.

Two GPUs: one holds the policy, one holds a vLLM instance for periodic validation. The handout gives you the plumbing, and you should copy it rather than reinvent it. init_vllm() monkeypatches two things borrowed from TRL — torch.distributed.get_world_size forced to 1 so vLLM will sit on a chosen device, and a memory-profiling assertion disabled because it does not hold in this setup. load_policy_into_vllm_instance() pushes policy.state_dict() straight into llm.llm_engine.model_executor.driver_worker.model_runner.model before every evaluation, so vLLM scores the current weights rather than the checkpoint it booted with. Forgetting that call gives you a validation curve that is flat at the base model's accuracy forever — a genuinely confusing failure, since training loss looks fine. Gradient clipping at 1.0 throughout.

5 · Expert iteration for MATH

The bridge between SFT and RL, and the cheapest way to see that a verifier is worth more than a teacher. Algorithm 2 wraps SFT in an outer loop: freeze a copy of the policy, sample G solutions per question from it, score them, throw away everything with reward 0, finetune on what survives, repeat. It is STaR exactly, and the same idea appears as Cobbe et al. 2021, Language Model Cascades and ReST in the handout's citation list. L16 calls it the RFT baseline in the DeepSeekMath comparison plot, which is the right frame: expert iteration is the strongest thing you can do without a policy gradient, and GRPO has to beat it to justify itself.

Problem (expert_iteration_experiment): Run expert iteration on the MATH dataset · 2 points · 6 H100 hrs

Deliverable: validation curves for at least two rollout counts × two epoch counts, a model above 15% validation accuracy, a two-sentence comparison against SFT and across EI steps, and an entropy-over-training plot.

Run on MATH/train.jsonl with n_ei_steps = 5, sweeping G (rollouts per question) and the number of SFT epochs per EI step. Both knobs trade the same currency. Larger G means more rollouts per question and therefore more surviving correct traces from hard questions — the coverage that makes EI work at all — at linear generation cost. More epochs per step squeezes harder on the traces you already have, at the cost of overfitting the model to its own outputs. The entropy plot is the requested deliverable because it shows the mechanism directly: EI is self-distillation, so entropy falls, and the interesting question is whether it falls faster than accuracy rises. Compare to the entropy curve you get from GRPO later — the handout explicitly asks you to in grpo_off_policy_sweep.

Two operational notes. Pass min_tokens=4 to SamplingParams; without it vLLM will occasionally return an empty string, which becomes an empty response mask, which becomes a division by zero, which becomes a NaN that quietly poisons the run. And keep the stop=["</answer>"] setup from §3 — the handout repeats the instruction here because people drop it when they switch from the eval path to the rollout path. Gradient clipping at 1.0 again.

  • STaR: Bootstrapping Reasoning With Reasoning — Zelikman et al. (2022) · the algorithm you are implementing
  • Thinking Fast and Slow with Deep Learning and Tree Search — Anthony et al. (2017) · where "expert iteration" gets its name

6 · Primer on policy gradients

No problems in this section — it is four pages of derivation you need before §7 makes sense, based on Spinning Up and Nathan Lambert's RLHF Book. The framing: a causal LM is a categorical stochastic policy, with the text prefix as state and the next token as action; the environment is deterministic (the next state is the old prefix plus the emitted token) and rewards are terminal only — 1 if the finished solution grades correct, 0 otherwise, nothing in between.

From there: the REINFORCE identity ∇J = E[Σ_t ∇ log π(a_t|s_t) · R(τ)], derived in six lines from the log-derivative trick plus the observation that ρ₀, P and R do not depend on θ. Then baselines — subtract any b(s_t) that depends only on state and the estimator stays unbiased, because the expected score function is zero, while the variance drops. Then off-policy: REINFORCE demands fresh rollouts for every single gradient step, which is absurd when generation dominates the cost, so you importance-weight by π_θ(a|s)/π_θold(a|s) and accept the approximation, which is fine as long as the two policies stay close (Degris et al. 2013).

One paragraph in §6.5 is worth memorizing, because it explains why every deliverable in §8 asks for reward curves and not loss curves:

pg_loss is not a loss in the canonical sense—it's not meaningful to report pg_loss on the train or validation set as an evaluation metric, and a good validation pg_loss doesn't indicate that our model is generalizing well. The pg_loss is really just some scalar such that when we call pg_loss.backward(), the gradients we obtain through backprop are the approximate policy gradient.§6.5, "A note on policy gradient 'losses'"

7 · Group Relative Policy Optimization

GRPO's one idea: get your baseline for free by sampling a group. Sample G solutions to the same question, and the mean reward across that group is an estimate of V(s) for that prompt — no learned value network to train, no second set of weights resident on the GPU, no critic that can be wrong. Advantage A⁽ⁱ⁾ = (r⁽ⁱ⁾ − mean(r)) / (std(r) + advantage_eps), per DeepSeekMath and R1. The advantage is constant across every token of a response, which is why it arrives shaped (batch_size, 1) and broadcasts along the sequence.

Algorithm 3 is the loop: for each GRPO step, sample a batch of questions, snapshot π_old ← π, sample G outputs per question, reward them, group-normalize, then take n_train_steps_per_rollout_batch gradient steps on the GRPO-Clip objective. The handout notes in a footnote that this is a special case of DeepSeekMath's GRPO with a verified reward function, no KL term, and no iterative reference or reward model update — the KL term is dropped because in the staff's experiments and in the literature it made no difference to performance while costing a resident reference model's worth of memory.

The clipping is PPO's, and the handout's case analysis is the clearest way to hold it. When A > 0, the objective is min(ratio, 1+ε)·A: you are rewarded for making the token more likely, but the reward saturates once the ratio exceeds 1+ε, so there is no gradient incentive to run away from π_old. When A < 0, symmetric: you push the token down but stop caring below 1−ε. Clipping only matters when π_θ has had a chance to drift from π_old — that is, when you take more than one gradient step per rollout batch.

Problem (compute_group_normalized_rewards): Group normalization · 2 points

Deliverable: score a flat list of rollouts, reshape into groups, normalize within each group, return normalized rewards, raw rewards and metadata.

The bookkeeping is the whole problem. rollout_responses arrives flat, of length rollout_batch_size = n_prompts_per_rollout_batch × group_size, with repeated_ground_truths the same length because each question's answer is repeated group_size times. Reshape to (n_prompts, group_size), subtract the per-group mean, and — if normalize_by_std — divide by the per-group std plus advantage_eps. Flatten back. Get the reshape order wrong (interleaving groups instead of blocking them) and you will normalize across questions instead of within them, which produces a plausible-looking tensor and an advantage signal that is pure noise.

The normalize_by_std flag is the Dr. GRPO ablation baked into the API: Liu et al. 2025 argue that dividing by the group std up-weights questions where the group barely disagreed — the too-easy and too-hard ones, where std is near zero — so they drop the division entirely and use A = r − mean(r). You implement both and test the difference in grpo_group_standard_deviation.

One repo wart to know: the adapter's return annotation reads tuple[torch.Tensor, dict[str, float]] but the docstring and both tests unpack three values — (advantages, raw_rewards, metadata). The annotation is stale; follow the docstring.

Problem (compute_naive_policy_gradient_loss): Naive policy gradient · 1 point

Deliverable: the per-token loss −A_t · log p_θ(o_t | q, o_<t).

A multiply and a negate: -raw_rewards_or_advantages * policy_log_probs, with the (batch, 1) scalar broadcasting over (batch, seq). Problem (compute_grpo_clip_loss): GRPO-Clip loss · 2 points

Deliverable: the per-token clipped surrogate, plus metadata recording which tokens clipped.

Form the ratio in log space — ratio = torch.exp(policy_log_probs - old_log_probs), never a division of probabilities — then -torch.min(ratio * advantages, ratio.clamp(1-cliprange, 1+cliprange) * advantages). Note the negation applies to the whole min, and the min is over the two products, not over the ratios: clipping the ratio first and multiplying afterwards gives a different (wrong) answer whenever the advantage is negative, which is the classic PPO implementation bug and the reason there are two tests here at different clip ranges. The metadata dict should record, per token, whether the clipped branch won — that is your clip fraction, the single best diagnostic for how far off-policy you have drifted.

The two tests, test_compute_grpo_clip_loss_large_cliprange and ..._small_cliprange, exist to catch exactly the sign/order confusion above: with a large clip range the clipping is inert and a broken implementation can still match, so the small-range snapshot is the one that actually discriminates.

Problem (grpo_microbatch_train_step): Microbatch train step · 3 points

Deliverable: per-token loss → masked mean over the sequence → mean over the batch → divide by accumulation steps → backward().

Both are quiet failures. Problem (think_about_length_normalization): Think about length normalization · 1 point

Deliverable: pros and cons of masked_mean versus masked_normalize, reasoned out before running anything, with settings where one looks better.

The handout hands you a worked numerical example from the RLHF Book: a batch of two responses, one 4 tokens and one 7, all ratios 1 and all advantages 2. Under masked_mean both sequences yield 2.0 and the per-token gradients come out 0.25 for the short sequence and 0.1429 for the long one. The argument for masked_mean is that it is per-sequence unbiased and keeps a single rambling rollout from dominating the batch. Problem (sst_baseline): Zero-shot SimpleSafetyTests baseline · 4 points

Four points for reading ten examples, which tells you how seriously the course takes the habit. The mix is UltraChat-200K plus SafetyTunedLlamas, flattened to single-turn {prompt, response} pairs. Problem (data_loading): Implement data loading · 3 points

Deliverable: a packed PyTorch Dataset and a batching function over it.

Part (a): format each pair with alpaca_sft.prompt, tokenize, concatenate all documents into one stream with the Llama end-of-text token between them, then chop into consecutive non-overlapping chunks of seq_length, dropping the final short chunk. __getitem__ returns {"input_ids", "labels"}, both (seq_length,), with labels the inputs shifted by one — which means the chunk you slice for a given index needs seq_length + 1 tokens available. Packing rather than padding is a throughput decision: no wasted positions, at the cost of documents bleeding across chunk boundaries. Part (b) is the batcher, with shuffle, one pass = one epoch.

The test compares your output against a stored fixture element by element, so ordering and boundary handling must match exactly, and it separately asserts that shuffle=True produces a different sequence than shuffle=False — shuffle the documents before concatenation, as the docstring says, not the chunks afterward.

Problem (mmlu_sft): Evaluate SFT on MMLU · 4 points

Deliverable: throughput versus baseline, accuracy versus baseline, and an error analysis over ten wrong predictions comparing the two models qualitatively.

One rule governs all four §5 evaluations and is easy to miss: drop the zero-shot system prompt and format everything with the Alpaca template you trained on instead. For MMLU and GSM8K that means nesting twice — format the task prompt with the example, then put that formatted string into the Alpaca {instruction} slot. Evaluate a finetuned model with the wrong wrapper and you measure the mismatch, not the model. The throughput question is not filler either: an instruction-tuned model stops rambling and starts terminating, so examples-per-second usually goes up sharply, and the reason is worth a sentence.

Problem (alpaca_eval_sft): Evaluate SFT on AlpacaEval · 4 points

Deliverable: throughput, winrate and LC winrate against the baseline, and ten dispreferred examples analysed.

This is where instruction tuning should show its largest single gain, because the benchmark measures exactly what you trained for. Problem (red_teaming): Red-team the instruction-tuned model · 4 points

Deliverable: three misuse categories beyond those already discussed, plus three documented red-teaming attempts on your own model.

Interactive rather than scripted, and the write-up wants method and honesty, not a scoreboard: what you tried, how long you tried it, which strategies you used, whether you succeeded. The framing follows Ganguli et al. 2022 — red-teaming is an evaluation method for understanding failure, not a compliance checkbox. Problem (look_at_hh): Inspect HH preference data · 2 points

Deliverable: a loader for the Anthropic HH dataset, plus 2–4 sentences on three "helpful" and three "harmless" examples and whether you agree with the annotators.

Four gzipped JSON-lines files on main at data/hh: harmless-base, helpful-base, helpful-online, helpful-rejection-sampled (training splits only). Each line has a chosen and a rejected conversation sharing a prefix. Three processing rules: drop multi-turn conversations where the human sent more than one message, because those diverge past the shared prompt and are no longer a clean pairwise comparison; split each example into instruction plus chosen/rejected responses; and remember which file each came from, since the write-up compares the two categories. gzip and json are all you need.

The write-up question is the good one. Anthropic deliberately declined to define "helpful" or "harmless", leaving it to annotator judgment, so the labels encode a distribution of opinions rather than a rule. You will find pairs where the difference is obvious and pairs where you would have chosen the other way — and that is the actual epistemic status of the preference signal DPO is about to optimize.

Problem (dpo_loss): DPO loss · 2 points

Deliverable: the per-instance DPO loss for one (prompt, chosen, rejected) triple under a policy and a frozen reference.

The whole of DPO in one function: −log σ(β·[log π_θ(y_w|x) − log π_ref(y_w|x)] − β·[log π_θ(y_l|x) − log π_ref(y_l|x)]). The derivation in §6.1 is worth following because it explains why this is legitimate — the optimal policy for a reward model can be inverted to express the reward in terms of the policy, and when you plug that into the Bradley-Terry reward-model loss the intractable partition function Z(x) cancels between the two responses. No reward model, no sampling, no RL loop; just four forward passes.

Three implementation details the test enforces. Format prompt and response with the Alpaca template and append the EOS token after each response — forget it and the arithmetic is close but not equal. Sum log-probs over the whole concatenated string rather than conditioning on the prompt; the supplement notes the prompt's contribution cancels in the difference under the same model, so unconditional sums are equivalent and simpler. And return the loss on the same device as the policy — the two models are expected to be on different GPUs.

  • tests/adapters.py · run_compute_per_instance_dpo_loss — (lm, lm_ref, tokenizer, beta: float, prompt: str, response_chosen: str, response_rejected: str) -> torch.Tensor
  • tests/test_dpo.py — test_per_instance_dpo_loss uses the checked-in tiny-gpt2 / tiny-gpt2-ref fixtures and the gpt2 tokenizer with beta=0.5, and asserts torch.isclose(loss, 0.5785, atol=1e-4) — an exact number, so template and EOS handling must match. Runs on CPU, no cluster needed.

Problem (dpo_training): DPO training · 4 points · 1 B200 hr

Deliverable: a DPO training script and a validation-accuracy screenshot, then AlpacaEval, SimpleSafetyTests, GSM8K and MMLU evaluations of the result.

One epoch over HH starting from your SFT checkpoint. The suggested path trades throughput for simplicity: two GPUs holding two copies of the instruction-tuned model (one frozen as π_ref), no batching at all — one example at a time, because four forward passes through an 8B model is already a lot of memory — with gradient accumulation supplying the effective batch size. RMSprop rather than AdamW, following the original DPO work, because AdamW's optimizer state is unaffordable here without quantization tricks.

Track the implicit reward model's classification accuracy on validation: for each pair, check whether the chosen response has higher log-probability than the rejected one under π_θ. That single number is your training signal made legible, and it is what selects the checkpoint.

Parts (b) through (d) are the payoff. AlpacaEval and SimpleSafetyTests should improve, since HH contains exactly the helpfulness and refusal behaviour they test. GSM8K and MMLU are the control, and the question is whether they fall — the alignment tax, observed in the original HH paper and much of the alignment literature since. Whether you see it, and how large it is at 8B on one epoch, is the actual finding.
