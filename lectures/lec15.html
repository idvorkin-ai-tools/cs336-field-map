TL;DR — Fourteen lectures built a model that can do things; this one is about getting it to do them on request. Hashimoto walks the InstructGPT pipeline in two halves: supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations, then optimization against a learned reward. The recurring surprise is that post-training data quality is not what your intuition says it is — factually correct, deeply-sourced demonstrations can make a model worse, because you are mostly teaching the shape of an answer rather than its content. The second half reframes the LM from a distribution to be matched into a policy to be maximized, writes down the Bradley–Terry-plus-KL objective everyone actually optimizes, sketches PPO, and then derives DPO — which collapses the whole RL apparatus into a supervised log-loss by assuming the policy class is unrestricted. If you remember one thing: SFT extracts behaviors the pre-trained model already has; it is a bad tool for installing behaviors it doesn't.

This is the first of three lectures on post-training, and it exists because the course's story so far stops one step short of anything useful. The scaling, systems and data lectures get you a GPT-3 — a model that has, somewhere in its parameters, the ability to reason, summarize, translate and code, and no reliable way of being asked to. The gap between that and ChatGPT is neither more parameters nor more tokens; it is a comparatively tiny amount of carefully chosen data plus a change in what you are optimizing. Lecture 15 is the anatomy of that step, and it is deliberately structured to mirror the InstructGPT paper because — as Hashimoto keeps pointing out — the industry's pipeline in 2025 is still recognizably that 2022 diagram.

Three ways to make instruction data, and what each one teaches

Hashimoto opens the SFT half by putting three datasets side by side, not because you need to know their contents but because they represent three different bets about where demonstrations come from. FLAN is the recycling bet: take the accumulated pile of supervised NLP datasets — question answering, topic classification, data-to-text generation, the Enron email corpus — and mechanically rewrite each into an instruction and a response. It is enormous and nearly free. It also reads like nothing a person would ever type into a chat box; you can see the surgery, and the responses are frequently one word. OpenAssistant is the volunteer bet: after ChatGPT shipped, enthusiasts sat down and wrote long, careful, human answers. High quality, small, and — as the lecture argues at length — quality that turns out to be a trap. Alpaca is the bootstrap bet: seed a small set of human instructions, let a language model expand it, and let a stronger model fill in the answers. Its prompts look like real chat inputs; its answers are always long-form prose.

The pedagogically expensive move is that he then makes the room do the job. Five minutes, one prompt, write the demonstration. The results are the point: mostly short, mostly thin, a couple obviously pasted from ChatGPT, at least one troll. Then he puts a GPT-4o answer on the same prompt next to them — structured, thorough, immediately better. The lesson isn't "humans are bad." It's that the thing labs pay millions for is not annotation but incentive design: getting a time-constrained worker to produce the top answer rather than the median one is a hard operational problem, and the moment a model can produce the top answer for a fraction of a cent, the economics of the whole pipeline invert. That inversion is why the second half of the lecture ends up talking about AI feedback rather than crowdworkers.

Style is not a cosmetic variable

Before the interesting result, a warning about measurement. Across the 2023 wave of instruction datasets, response length varies by an order of magnitude, and preference evaluation is badly sensitive to it: both human raters and LM judges pick the longer answer something like 60–70% of the time, and both like bullet points. If your eval is a win rate, you are measuring formatting with a large factuality-shaped error bar. The saving grace is that these style effects don't transfer to closed-form benchmarks — MMLU barely notices which instruction set you used. So run both kinds of eval and treat disagreement as signal: a change that moves win rate and leaves benchmarks flat changed your model's voice, not its ability.

The counterintuitive rule: correct data can hurt

This is the section worth the price of admission. Take the OpenAssistant example the lecture keeps returning to: a request to explain monopsony in economics, answered with a thorough paragraph ending in a real, correctly-formatted academic citation (Bivens & Mishel, 2013). It is factually right and richly sourced — by every naive quality metric, an excellent training example. Now ask what gradient descent extracts from it. Two things happen simultaneously. One: the model strengthens an association between "monopsony" and that citation. Two: the model learns a format rule — when asked something that looks scholarly, terminate with a reference.

If the pre-trained model already knows that citation, mechanism one dominates and everything is fine. If it doesn't, mechanism two is the only thing it can actually satisfy, and you have just trained a citation fabricator. Token prediction offers no way to decline; the response has a shape, the shape has slots, and the slots must be filled.

"…the lesser of the two errors is to say hallucinating is less bad for my loss than not making up the reference at all."— Tatsunori Hashimoto, 21:51

A student pushes back well here — surely learning to cite is correct behavior, and the fabrication is separately fixable with better memory or a retrieval tool. Hashimoto grants the point for a well-resourced system and holds the line on the mechanism: whenever your SFT data is meaningfully more advanced than what the base model can do, you are teaching a shortcut, not a skill. Two consequences fall out. Distillation from a teacher stronger than your student carries this risk by construction. So does hiring annotators who know more than your model. And the practical fix is not better SFT — it is to determine what the model knows and only train the answer when it knows it, which is a fundamentally on-policy operation and therefore a job for the RL half of the pipeline. That thread is picked up in L16. Empirically the argument has support: Gekhman et al. find that fine-tuning examples carrying genuinely new facts are learned slowly and, as they are learned, push hallucination rates up roughly linearly.

Safety tuning is the mirror image, and the reason the "don't add data" heuristic isn't the whole story. Here you are installing a behavior — refusal — and it works with astonishingly little: on the order of 500 examples mixed into an otherwise normal instruction set shifts a model's safety profile substantially. The catch is the second-order effect. Push refusal training too hard and the model starts refusing prompts that merely resemble unsafe ones; the lecture's example, "how can I kill a Python process," is exactly the surface pattern a shallow refusal classifier learns. Safety tuning is therefore a curation problem with a two-sided error, not a data-volume problem.

Carry this away: before adding anything to an SFT mix, ask which of two things it will teach — the content, or the shape. If your base model can't produce the content on its own, you will get the shape and a plausible-looking fabrication in the content slot. That is one rule that explains the "don't fine-tune on tail knowledge" folklore, the distillation-gap failure mode, and why safety data (pure shape, no knowledge) works at 500 examples while factual data at 50,000 can still make things worse.

Midtraining: SFT is being eaten by pre-training

The method question — how do you actually fine-tune? — has a boring answer and an interesting one. Boring: it's gradient descent on (instruction, response) pairs, and in an academic setting that genuinely is the whole thing. Interesting: if you have frontier-lab compute, the boundary between pre-training and instruction tuning stops being real. Instruction data is a token sequence like any other, so you can mix it into pre-training — specifically into the decay phase of a warmup–stable–decay schedule, where the learning rate is annealing and the loss is dropping fast. Then you do a short conventional SFT round on top.

The lecture's public evidence is MiniCPM's two-stage recipe: stage one is a pure pre-training mixture (Common Crawl, code, the Pile, Dolma); the decay stage keeps some of that but folds in Wikipedia, code SFT, UltraChat, Stack Exchange QA, Evol-Instruct, OSS-Instruct. Hashimoto is explicit that this is common knowledge inside labs and almost entirely undocumented, surfacing mainly in Chinese open releases. The payoff is scaling instruction tuning without catastrophic forgetting — no tiny step sizes or regularization tricks to protect the pre-trained weights, because the instruction data was never a separate phase to forget.

His side comment matters more than it sounds: the term "base model" is now unreliable. A modern release labelled "base" has usually been through midtraining and has therefore seen instruction data. If you are running a research comparison that depends on a clean pre-trained checkpoint, that assumption may simply be false, and you have no way to check.

From imitation to optimization

The second half opens with a conceptual switch that is worth slowing down for. SFT is generative modeling: there exists some reference distribution p*(y|x) — some blend of internet text and annotator writing — and you fit p̂ to it. That framing requires samples from p*, which is precisely the expensive thing. RLHF discards the distribution entirely. You want any policy p̂(y|x) maximizing Ep̂[R(y,x)] for a reward you can measure.

"LMs are not necessarily a model for some underlying distribution. They are policies that give us good rewards."— Tatsunori Hashimoto, 43:06

Two arguments justify paying for that switch. The first is cost, and the slide's toy accounting for a 7B model makes it vivid:

 base modelSFT (one-time)pairwise feedbackRLeval
compute$300k$100$100$100$0
annotation$0$25k$4k$0$50

Every compute cell after pre-training is rounding error. The real money is annotation, and demonstrations cost roughly six times what preferences cost for the same budget line — because writing an expert answer is harder than ranking two. The second argument is subtler and, to my mind, the stronger one: the generator–validator gap. In a news-summarization benchmark from Hashimoto's group, some annotators preferred model-written summaries to their own. One freelance writer, interviewed afterward, said that when asked to write they felt obliged toward flowery prose, but reading the AI's version, it simply read better. So preferences are not merely cheaper than demonstrations — for open-ended tasks they can be a higher-quality signal, because people's taste outruns their production.

What preference data is actually measuring

Which makes it worth asking what a preference label contains. The lecture digs up two real annotation guidelines — InstructGPT's (helpful, truthful, harmless, with bullets about clear language, internationalization, asking for clarification) and a leaked Google Bard rubric with near-identical structure plus an explicit style box. Then it runs exercise two: five pairwise judgments, five minutes. Of roughly 27 students who finished, about one fact-checked anything and a couple checked the math — and the longer option in each pair was, by construction, the one with hallucinations quietly inserted. The longer option won. These are motivated graduate students with no time pressure beyond the classroom; Bard's annotators reportedly got about a minute per item.

The failure modes compound rather than average out. Annotator identity leaks straight into model behavior — Santurkar et al. traced InstructGPT's opinion distributions shifting toward the demographics in the paper's own annotator appendix (Filipino and Bangladeshi contractors, 17% American), and InstructGPT's entire preference dataset came from about 40 people sourced through Scale and Upwork. Hosking, Blunsom and Bartolo show crowdworkers weight formatting heavily and factuality barely, while expert annotators do the opposite — same rubric, different signal. And the modern contamination: annotators paste the task into GPT-4 and return its answer, with studies observing 95%+ agreement with GPT-4 from individual workers. The ethics of the outsourced-annotation supply chain get their own slide, and they should.

Given all that, the field's turn to LM-generated feedback reads less like cost-cutting and more like capitulation to a measurement problem. GPT-4 agrees with humans about as often as humans agree with each other, and correlates near-perfectly with human rankings at the system level. UltraFeedback, Zephyr, OLMo and Tülu 3 all run on it; Constitutional AI planted the flag. Hashimoto notes that the Hugging Face team building Zephyr started convinced that well-paid human annotators would beat AI feedback and concluded otherwise mid-project. Two cautions ride along: models show a detectable self-preference for their own outputs (fatal if you use the same model as judge and policy), and AI feedback makes models longer, so length remains the confounder that never goes away.

The objective, PPO, and the DPO shortcut

The objective everyone optimizes is InstructGPT's equation 2, and it has three terms. First, the learned reward rθ(x,y). Second, a KL penalty log(πRL(y|x) / πSFT(y|x)) — don't wander far from where you started. Third, an optional pre-training term (weighted γ) that keeps some pre-training loss in the mix; most people skip it, and the KL anchor has survived every subsequent redesign.

The reward itself rests on a modeling assumption worth stating plainly, because it is easy to use without noticing. The Bradley–Terry model posits that every possible sequence has a latent scalar value R; a human comparing A and B computes R(A) − R(B) and flips a coin biased by that difference through a logistic. You never observe R, only noisy pairwise draws — so you fit rθ to reproduce the comparisons, then maximize it. Every consistency assumption about human preference you might worry about lives in that one sentence.

PPO gets one slide and a promise to return in L16. The three-step compression: (1) the policy gradient / REINFORCE identity, ∇θEpθ[R(z)] = Epθ[R(z)∇θ log pθ(z)] — upweight sequences with positive reward, downweight negative — which is correct and has hopeless variance; (2) replace R with an advantage, which is R minus any state-dependent baseline, a subtraction that leaves the gradient unbiased and cuts the variance; and to reuse one batch of rollouts for several gradient steps you need importance-weighting corrections plus a trust region (TRPO); (3) PPO replaces the explicit trust-region constraint with clipping the probability ratios, which achieves the stay-close effect far more cheaply.

"…we have debated but decided against having you implement PPO, because it will be suffering."— Tatsunori Hashimoto, 71:00

That line is the segue. The community spent 2023 asking whether the RL could be removed. The lecture lists the attempts that mostly failed: control tokens (SFT on both members of each pair, prepending [GOOD]/[BAD], then conditioning on GOOD); training only on the preferred response; training a reward model and fine-tuning on its picks; and best-of-n sampling, taking the top of 1024 candidates — which works acceptably but is an inference-time tax, not a fix.

DPO is what stuck, and the derivation is three moves. Start from the same reward-plus-KL objective. Make a nonparametric assumption: let π range over all policies rather than the ones your network can express. Then the maximizer has a closed form — proportional to πref(y|x)·exp(r(x,y)/β) — which you can invert to solve for the reward implied by any policy. That inversion is the whole trick: reward and policy become two names for one object, so you can stop training a reward model and parametrize the reward through the policy directly. Substitute that implied reward into the Bradley–Terry likelihood from Stiennon et al., and the RL problem becomes maximum likelihood on the pairwise comparisons — a supervised log-loss, no rollouts, no outer loop, no reward network. Mechanically it does what you'd want by hand: positive gradient on the chosen response, negative on the rejected, each scaled by how wrong the implied reward model currently is on that pair.

Where the lecture hedges, note it. On the AlpacaFarm controlled comparison DPO matched their PPO implementation — but Hashimoto is careful that "PPO also works, and sometimes better," and that RL empirical results are unusually contingent on setup details. The slide deck's last three sections — DPO variants from Tülu 3 (SimPO, length-normalized DPO), reward overoptimization, and RLHF-induced mode collapse and loss of calibration — were not reached; the lecture ran a few minutes long and stops at the DPO derivation, with the remainder deferred to the start of L16. If you are reading the slides alongside the video, that's where they diverge.

Exercises

  1. Reproduce the annotation experiment on yourself — Pick five prompts with checkable content (two arithmetic, two with factual claims, one open-ended). For each, generate two candidate answers from any model, then hand-edit the longer one to insert one false claim. Give yourself sixty seconds per pair — Bard's reported budget — and record your picks. Then verify at leisure. A good answer reports your own error rate, notes whether you caught the arithmetic or the facts more reliably, and states what that implies about the reward model you would have trained from your labels.
  2. Shape vs. content, measured code — Build two 200-example SFT sets from the same prompts: set A answers with correct content and no citations; set B answers with correct content plus a real citation. Fine-tune a small base model (1B or under) on each. (1) Hold out 50 prompts about entities the base model demonstrably knows and 50 about obscure entities — establish "knows" by sampling the base model ten times and checking consistency. (2) Fine-tune both runs with identical hyperparameters. (3) Generate on both held-out sets. (4) Measure citation-emission rate and citation-validity rate separately. A good answer shows the emission rate rising uniformly while validity collapses on the obscure half — the two mechanisms of the monopsony example, separated.
  3. Derive DPO without looking — From the objective maxπ Eπ[r(x,y)] − β·KL(π ‖ πref), derive the closed-form optimal policy over unrestricted π, invert it for r, and substitute into the Bradley–Terry log-likelihood. A good answer identifies exactly where the partition function Z(x) cancels (it appears identically in both branches of the pairwise difference), and states plainly what the nonparametric assumption buys and what it costs once πθ is a finite network that cannot represent the optimum.
  4. Audit an open preference dataset for length code — Load a public preference set (UltraFeedback or the Anthropic HH data). (1) Compute the fraction of pairs where the chosen response is the longer one. (2) Fit a classifier that predicts the label from response length alone and report its accuracy. (3) Re-run on a length-balanced subsample. A good answer gives the length-only baseline as a number and says what fraction of a reward model trained on this data could be explained by that one feature.
